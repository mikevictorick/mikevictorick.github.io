— Case study 01

AI Adoption Training for District Educators

— Business problem
A district mandate to build a foundation for AI adoption among educators. Staff had no shared starting point for using AI to reduce workload, and leadership needed a way to introduce it at scale.
— Audience
District-wide and mixed: classroom teachers across grade levels and subjects, school administrators, and district instructional leadership.
— Constraints
One 45-minute session. No advance information about attendees' subjects or AI experience. No built-in follow-up time. The content had to work for complete beginners and advanced users in the same room.
— My role
Sole instructional designer and facilitator. I hold SchoolAI's Educator Certification (Level 2). The district approved the session from my brief, and I produced the design, content, and delivery myself.
— Approach
I worked backward from one outcome: every attendee leaves with two working AI prompts built from the TEKS they teach, one for a project-based assignment and one for a formative assignment. A three-path structure (beginner, intermediate, advanced) let people pick the level that fit them, and all three paths ended at the same outcome. I taught one transferable habit, asking the AI to improve its own prompt, so the skill would outlast the session. I ran the session through with a fellow educator, revised from their notes, and sent it to the district for approval.
— Deliverables
A slide deck and a one-page takeaway with both prompt templates. The originals are district material and I no longer have them, so the samples below are recreated.
— Evidence of impact
I followed up individually with attendees using the sign-in sheet. Most reported using at least one AI-assisted workflow within the week, and the feedback was strongly positive. The district's lead media specialist also sought me out afterward to say the session was practical. I did not run a formal survey, so these are reports I heard directly.
— What I learned
Designing for adults is a different job than designing for students. Adults often carry real apprehension about new technology, even when they use it daily. Running the session through with a peer before delivery showed me the value of outside review when you are designing for an audience you cannot fully see in advance.

— Case study 02

Canvas LMS Course Templates and Reusable AI Workflows

— Business problem
Losing Canvas's built-in design tool removed any way to build course pages with a consistent look and structure. Without a system, each teacher built pages individually, and course content across the program came out inconsistent and messy.
— Audience
Three teachers piloting the system and giving ongoing feedback, plus other teachers across the district who saw it and adapted it with their own AI tools, with mixed results.
— Constraints
Canvas allows HTML but not CSS, so the templates had to be built from structural HTML. That is a real barrier for teachers who are uncomfortable with code, since any edit risks breaking the layout. I also had to work around district media policy and use the district's designated video system instead of direct embeds.
— My role
Fully self-directed. I identified the efficiency and systems gap myself and took ownership of solving it without being assigned.
— Approach
I used AI to build a program-specific brand system, giving it Canvas's allowed HTML structure and each module's required elements (objectives, TEKS, standard lesson-planning components). I standardized on two reusable page templates, an Assignment Page and a Submission Page, to replace ad hoc page building. I packaged the process into two modular AI skills: one that sets up a course's brand and folder structure, and a second that uses that structure to generate standardized Canvas pages. That made the system self-service for other teachers.
— Deliverables
Two reusable AI skills (brand and structure setup, and Canvas page generation) plus the two standardized page templates they produce. The originals are school material, so the samples below are recreated.
— Evidence of impact
Of the pilot group, one teacher has made the system their core workflow. One adopted it and adapted it into their own version for building Canvas pages. One is still working through it with my direct support, which has given me a real case study in adoption barriers for less tech-comfortable users.
— What I learned
People find uses for a tool beyond the problem it was built to solve, which is why I test across different users and situations instead of one scenario. Delivering a reusable tool also takes more than building it. Adoption needs ongoing support, not a handoff.

— Case study 03

Ad Hoc Technology Enablement: Generative AI Support for School Staff

— Business problem
Staff across the school kept hitting different walls with Gemini and other AI tools: prompting and iteration technique, building lesson handouts, staying under usage limits, and basic orientation. No structured training or support channel existed for any of it.
— Audience
Five staff members in distinct roles (CTE lead, counselor, instructional coach, 9th grade English teacher, coach), each with a different use case and skill level.
— Constraints
Requests arrived unscheduled, with only minutes available per person alongside a full teaching load. Each conversation started with diagnosing the problem, the person's skill level, and their learning style on the spot.
— My role
Informal go-to resource, unassigned and solo. Colleagues sought me out directly after learning I hold Claude, SchoolAI, and MagicSchool certifications.
— Approach
I used one coaching method. Start with the outcome the person wants. Find out what they have already tried and which tool they used, and suggest a better-fit tool when there is one. Guide with questions instead of taking over. Teach one repeatable starting move: describe the task and have the AI write the prompt. Follow up later to see whether anyone is stuck.
— Deliverables
A one-page AI tool guide for educators: which tool fits which job (MagicSchool, SchoolAI, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini with NotebookLM, and Brisk) and which to skip. It is built to send as a follow-up after a conversation.
— Evidence of impact
Of the five staff I supported, four reached their goal. Two got there on their own after a check-in. Two got there with guidance, and one of those now asks me for further tips. The fifth asked for hands-on help with a workflow that spans several tools and procedures.
— What I learned
Most support happens outside a structured setting, with no agenda or slide deck to carry the conversation. Credibility comes from how well you know the tools.
